## Finding

The capacity map is internally consistent as a set of attained rates for distinct operations. It does not establish a model-level speed prediction or an impossibility result. Its strongest architectural implications are narrower: native quantized CPU work must be evaluated with native service measurements rather than the unrelated FP32 FMA loop; GPU expert GEMM attainment varies materially with tested row shape; a prior logical transfer estimate nearly consumes the 1,000-token/s wall budget if every byte must traverse the measured host-USM path once; and the synthetic concurrency trace observed no copy/GEMM device-interval overlap under its recorded safe settings. Those findings justify measuring the actual route's shapes, bytes, returned-event/internal-kernel service and transfer dependencies. They do not show the current 32K route cannot meet a target, nor that queue reordering alone can.

## CPU denominator and scale

The native fixture uses IQ2_S GU and IQ4_NL Down weights. Its valid workload normalization counts one MAC as two dense-equivalent FLOPs; this is not an FP operation count. At NT4, complete direct service is 147.419 microseconds per active expert-token for the hot set and 148.362 microseconds for streaming; the existing five-worker pool plus host is 34.736 and 38.738 microseconds, respectively. These are per-token rates from the 24 closed clean timing receipts, with three fresh processes per NT/set. The NT4 direct hot/streaming medians differ by about 0.6%; the pool medians differ by about 11.5%. Hot and streaming use different expert subsets and work geometry, and neither result identifies cache, DRAM, spill or scheduling cause. R224's emitted NT4 stack spills remain static code evidence, not a measured cost.

The separate six-core register FP32 FMA control attained 0.818 TFLOP/s. Dividing native dense-equivalent FLOP/s by that value would compare different instruction streams and is invalid as utilization or roofline evidence. The FMA rate only characterizes the FMA loop, compiler and host. Similarly, native logical packed-weight bytes per second are bytes in the fixture's declared operands, not PMU-measured RAM traffic. No physical DRAM transaction count is present.

For scale only, applying the NT4 pool streaming median serially to all 48 × 10 = 480 routed expert-token jobs would give roughly 18.6 ms/token (about 54 token/s). That is a conditional multiplication of a microfixture service time, not a decoder prediction or proven bound: production prefill batches many tokens, actual per-expert row counts and execution scheduling differ, and the fixture uses deterministic synthetic activations. Its meaning is to show that a meaningful workload-normalized estimate must preserve job count, NT, active set, and topology.

## GPU expert shape and operation scope

The B570 controls report 52.69 TFLOP/s GU and 41.61 TFLOP/s Down for hot large-M GEMMs (M8192), versus 13.16/10.08 TFLOP/s at M80 with eight rotating synthetic weights and 25.78/20.03 TFLOP/s at M160. These are attained probe rates, not device maxima. The factor-of-about-two change from M80 to M160 demonstrates that one large-M rate cannot be assigned to every expert call. The M80/M160 controls do not provide the production routed-M histogram or current model's kernel mix; eight rotating weights do not prove cold cache. Therefore shape-specific points are useful capacity anchors, while the production compute time remains unqualified.

The routed GU/Down arithmetic check passes: for H=2,560, FF=640, topK=10 and 48 layers, the stated dense-equivalent work is 9,830,400 FLOPs per expert-token, 4.718592 GFLOP per model token, and 154.619 TFLOP over 32,768 tokens, excluding attention, shared experts, dequantization and other work. At 1,000 tokens/s this routed arithmetic alone averages 4.719 TFLOP/s. This arithmetic requirement is not an observed per-request service rate. Comparing it directly with M8192 or the FP16 synthetic GEMM rate would overstate evidence because actual expert shapes, dependency/dispatch costs and non-GEMM work are not measured in a matched 32K route.

The prefill service ledger qualifies returned routine events only. It does not establish that an event span includes every internal oneMKL kernel or measure exclusive GPU busy time. The separate full-262,144-position input validation is not established by a configured context length, and the latest actual 32K model performance/internal-span qualification remains open.

## PCIe / transfer budget

The recorded host-USM controls are one-way payload rates: RAM-to-GPU 6.447 GB/s and GPU-to-RAM 5.643 GB/s on 256 MiB transfers with host-inclusive completion. Pageable H2D is a different path at 4.605 GB/s. They are measured on this host/runtime and are not PCIe specification maxima or proof of saturation.

The older matched-32K route's approximately 190.2 GB of logical expert copies divided by the H2D host-USM control gives 29.5 s. A 1,000-token/s run over 32,768 tokens has a 32.768 s wall budget, leaving about 3.3 s under the explicit conditional that all 190.2 GB must be transferred once at that separate control rate. This is a useful warning about budget sensitivity, not a physical lower bound: it reuses an older route's logical byte count and a standalone control rate; current route bytes/reuse, exact transfer path, actual DMA service, transfer/cache behavior and overlap are not measured together. Logical payload does not equal physical PCIe traffic or establish that every historical copy reaches the link.

## Scheduling versus data volume

The synthetic concurrency PTI trace contains 144 8-MiB H2D commands and 3,168 GEMM commands. Its analyzed target copy and GEMM device intervals have zero intersection, including in the concurrent phase, with a closest cross-role gap of 5.084 microseconds. This is evidence of no overlap observed in that one profiling-perturbed run with its recorded safe runtime settings. Both PTI lanes are labeled `L0 Compute Engine<0,0>`; the label is a queue-group ordinal/index report, not proof of physical-engine exclusivity. The trace is synthetic, not the production ring or actual model route.

Three clean processes showed a modest and variable Down wall-time benefit for concurrent host submission (the observed serial/concurrent ratios vary by shape and process). This can coexist with zero observed device overlap if host submission/wait gaps changed, but the trace does not prove that mechanism. Neither result supports claiming that scheduling can hide the current transfer volume. Nor does zero overlap in this control prove overlap impossible in the production route. A measured matched-route event and transfer timeline is needed to decide whether changing dependencies helps; if device operations remain serialized, the byte/reuse or representation budget must carry the improvement.

The actual PLE capacity control similarly distinguishes application payload from underlying storage service: 1 MiB sequential requests reached 2.266 GB/s at 16 workers, while random 4 KiB reads reached 0.05099 GB/s at 16 workers. Whole-process pool counters show about 32 direct-DMU bytes per random application byte but about one per aligned sequential byte. These counter windows include startup, untimed final buffered references and pool-wide activity; they do not measure NAND bytes or attribute bytes to the shard. The results support testing record coalescing, not a causal claim about actual PLE or a hardware limit.

## Limits and next evidence boundary

The completed controls are a hardware-capacity map, not a joined model service trace. Current production shape histogram, current 32K route bytes/reuse, actual transfer service on that route, internal oneMKL kernel spans, and validated 262,144-position execution are not demonstrated by these artifacts. The existing zero-overlap and logical-byte evidence should therefore constrain hypotheses, not decide feasibility. A model-impossibility claim needs a matched complete workload and a proven unavoidable-work lower bound; neither follows from these operation controls.
