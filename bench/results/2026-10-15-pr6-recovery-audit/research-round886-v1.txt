# R886 — When hardware-supported 2:4 structured sparsity accelerates matrix inference end to end

**Purpose:** Knowledge collection. **Scope:** NVIDIA Ampere Sparse Tensor Cores and TensorRT evidence only; this is not an Arc, Intel, or Strata recommendation. Sources were checked on 2026-10-16. Prior work R778 (general SpMV/SpMM formats), R563/R564/R701 (dense Strata GEMMs), and R873 (dense ASIC dataflow) is excluded by metadata; their report bodies were not reopened.

## Question and short answer

When can a 2:4 weight mask improve end-to-end inference? On NVIDIA Ampere, only when the layer is eligible, the weight operand is packed, the sparse tactic wins for its shape, and pruning quality is recovered. A100 doubles peak math rate for supported operations, but the TensorRT 8.0 ResNeXt-101_32x8d example gains only about 10–18% model performance at batches 8–1024. TensorRT can also choose dense for eligible layers. The model chart omits timing methodology, so it is not a reproducible latency benchmark.

## Mechanism and eligibility

Ampere’s 2:4 pattern applies to groups of four values in one matrix operand: at least two are zero. Compression stores two value slots plus position metadata; the other GEMM operand and output remain dense. A group with more than two zeros still uses two slots, so extra zeros do not raise the skip rate. Regularity limits index overhead and avoids irregular accesses and load imbalance. The paper calculates about 44% less storage for 16-bit values (36 versus 64 bits per group) and 38% for 8-bit values. These are representation savings, not end-to-end speedups. [R1]

Sparse MMA skips multiply-add work for the zero entries. The A100 architecture whitepaper’s illustrative 16×16-by-16×8 half-precision operation takes N cycles with dense MMA and N/2 with Sparse MMA. It states roughly 2× storage/bandwidth reduction and 2× math throughput for the constrained operand. The original study gives A100 peak rates: FP16×FP16 with FP32 accumulation, 312 dense versus 624 sparse TOPS; BF16×BF16 with FP32 accumulation, 312 versus 624; INT8×INT8 with INT32 accumulation, 624 versus 1,248. TF32 has 156 versus 312 TOPS but uses a 1:2 pattern in the study’s description. These are A100 rates, not measurements on arbitrary Ampere GPUs and not model speedups. [R1, R2]

Eligibility is narrower than “half the weights are zero.” NVIDIA libraries require sparse matrix dimensions to be multiples of 16 for FP16/BF16 and 32 for INT8. The study excludes embeddings, inference-unused layers, and reductions that miss these multiples. TensorRT checks K groups for matrix multiply; for convolution, groups of four input channels per output channel and kernel position, with C divisible by four. TensorRT 10.16.1 documents FP16 or INT8 enablement; the Ampere study separately describes hardware FP16, BF16, INT8 and TF32. Hardware support alone does not prove library or layer eligibility. [R2, R4]

## Why matrix results do not equal inference results

The matrix study samples M/N/K shapes and reports that larger GEMMs approach 2× due to greater arithmetic intensity. Small shapes may be limited by setup, utilization, or non-matrix work. For language workloads, N can grow with sequence length times batch; N=10K at sequence 256 and batch 40 is an example, not a threshold. This is INT8 A100 GEMM performance using cuSPARSELt sparse versus cuBLAS dense, not end-to-end transformer inference. It does not establish results for other shapes, types, GPUs, or library versions. [R2]

For network evidence, NVIDIA’s July 2021 technical blog describes a ResNeXt-101_32x8d model imported as ONNX into TensorRT 8.0, built with FP16 and sparsity enabled, and measured on an A100. Its chart’s x-axis lists batch sizes 8, 32, 64, 128, 256, and 1024. Reading chart bars approximately, throughput relative to dense is 1.10×, 1.14×, 1.15×, 1.15×, 1.17×, and 1.18× respectively. The performance-per-watt bars are about 1.26×, 1.34×, 1.36×, 1.19×, 1.17×, and 1.20×. This supports a favorable regime: sparse performance advantage grows modestly with batch for this compute-heavy convolutional network, while lower-batch clock/power behavior yields a larger efficiency improvement around batch 32–64. The exact model accuracy table reports ImageNet Top-1 of 79.3% dense and 79.3% sparse, after a 2:4 pruning workflow and retraining. [R3]

These vendor chart readings are not an independent replication. The blog omits per-batch latency, warmup/repetitions, clocks/power settings, timing boundary, and tactic list, so only relative performance is available. Equal accuracy is specific to reported ResNeXt Top-1 of 79.3% dense and sparse. Other models differ: retrained ResNet-50 is 76.1% dense versus 76.2% sparse; MobileNet v2 is 71.55% dense versus 69.56% after basic pruning/fine-tuning, recovering to 71.56% with channel permutation. Pruning alone is not accuracy-preserving; the sparse model’s quality follows recovery training. [R2, R3]

The roughly 2× GEMM ceiling versus 1.10–1.18× model bars suggests that only part of network latency benefits, or that sparse kernels achieve less than peak on model shapes. This is an inference from separate measurements: the blog publishes neither per-layer timing nor tactic logs, so a precise Amdahl decomposition is unavailable. At batch 8 the chart shows roughly 10% performance improvement but 26% performance-per-watt; at batch 256 it shows about 17% for both. These are read-offs from a plot, not raw samples.

## Counterexample and tactic crossover

TensorRT 10.16.1 makes an important limitation explicit: a sparse tactic can be slower than the ordinary tactic, so the builder may choose dense even after a layer passes the structured-sparsity check. Its verbose example reports three eligible convolution layers but selects sparse tactics for only two. This is a concrete runtime counterexample to “eligible implies accelerated”: the remaining eligible layer is handled by a dense tactic. The documentation does not identify the GPU model, batch, layer dimensions, accuracy result, or measured latency for that example; no quantitative slowdown should be inferred. It also does not say which condition causes the losing tactic, only that it can be slower. [R4]

The decision boundary is per-layer tactic timing plus network timing, not nominal sparsity. Metadata, shape constraints, small matrix work, and unchanged dense layers can erase gains. TensorRT may build a mixed graph; its log distinguishes eligible from sparse-selected layers. Attribute speedup only after measuring the whole network, including layers still run dense. Peak rates and one GEMM measurement cannot replace that comparison.

## Accuracy and conversion costs

The proposed workflow trains dense, prunes two weights per group, then retrains with the mask fixed. Repeating training can roughly double training time, a preparation cost that may be amortized across deployment. Packed weights are required: the paper gives 2-bit metadata per value, or 12.5% overhead over 16-bit nonzeros and 25% over 8-bit, so savings are below 50%. TensorRT 8.0 says it compresses 2:4 weights automatically. If conversion occurs once at engine build, it is outside steady-state latency; if repeated per request, measure it. The blog reports no conversion time and does not say whether engine construction is in its chart. [R1, R2, R3]

## Transfer checklist for another accelerator (hypothesis framework only)

For another accelerator with explicit sparse-matrix hardware, verify: (1) pattern and operand axis; (2) supported types, accumulation, dimensions, and alignment; (3) whether the library emits sparse instructions; (4) per-layer sparse versus dense tactic choices; (5) kernel results across M/N/K, batch, and sequence; (6) full inference timings with matching boundaries; (7) model quality after documented pruning and retraining; (8) conversion amortization and memory use. This is a hypothesis checklist only; it predicts no capability or performance for Intel/Arc or other devices.
