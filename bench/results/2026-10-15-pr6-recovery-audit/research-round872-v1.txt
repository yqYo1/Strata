# GPU equijoin build/probe crossovers under key skew and memory limits

**Classification:** Knowledge collection. No implementation or adoption is proposed.

## Finding

There is no architecture-independent size ratio at which one GPU equijoin wins. The crossover is between irregular global-memory build/probe and the extra sequential passes and temporary storage of partitioning or sorting. A global hash join is attractive when its table fits comfortably, probes are moderate, and output is small; radix partitioning buys locality and bounds the working set at the price of several relation reads/writes; sort-merge buys balanced matching and ordered output but pays to sort both inputs. Once payload materialization dominates, comparing only hash-table or match-finding kernels can select the wrong plan.

For a build input of N_b rows, a rough direct-table capacity model is T ≈ N_b × slot_bytes / α, where α is target load factor and slot_bytes includes key/value and occupancy/alignment overhead. Peak memory also includes inputs, output, and scratch. A partitioned plan needs transformed arrays or buckets, histograms/offsets and output; sort-merge needs key/value sort buffers, sort scratch, and output. These are accounting models, not universal measured formulas: the papers use different layouts and allocation lifetimes.

For a many-to-many equijoin, output rows are Σ_k f_R(k) × f_S(k); one frequent key can create a quadratic result even if the build table is small. Duplicate multiplicity must be retained, so one table entry per key is insufficient without a list/range of all build rows. Partitioning or sort balancing can distribute work but cannot shrink result bytes. Count/scan/emit or bounded output batches handle unknown result size, with extra traversal and coordination.

## Algorithm and crossover comparison

| Plan | Build/transform and probe/merge | Space and skew behavior | Crossover evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct global GPU hash | Build on the smaller side, then probe. Avoids relation-wide partition passes, but accesses are irregular; high load factor and negative probes raise lookup work. | Table capacity is the critical allocation. Duplicate build keys need a multivalue representation. Hot-key insertion can contend and output can dominate. | The 2023 A100 study says cuDF's non-partitioned table loses to its partitioned join on tested narrow workloads due to random accesses. This does not prove every direct table loses; cache-fitting inputs and table design matter. |
| Radix-partitioned hash | Histogram/scatter both inputs into matching partitions, sometimes in multiple passes; build a small table per build partition and stream probes through it. | Adds temporary arrays/buckets, metadata, and read/write traffic. Oversized or hot partitions need subdivision or fallback; skewed bucket allocation can add atomic contention. | Rui and Tu report an older design's third pass at 32M tuples caused a runtime jump; their newer two-pass scheme avoided that cliff over a wider range. The 2023 PHJ beats direct cuDF in its narrow tests, but its bucket-chaining variant degrades under skew. |
| Sort-merge | Sort both key/value or key/row-ID arrays, then merge equal-key ranges. Merge-path/chunking assigns balanced work. | Needs sort buffers and multiple passes; wider values increase that traffic. Merge scheduling tolerates skew, but duplicate output remains. Reusable presorted inputs change the cost. | Rui and Tu measured sort-merge up to 26% faster than radix hash on their Titan X/Kepler tests. The later A100 study found partitioned hash generally faster, showing the crossover depends on hardware, primitive, layout, and timing boundary. |

## Phase-level evidence

Wu, Koutsoukos and Alonso evaluated in-memory primary-foreign-key joins on an A100 (40 GB, 1,555 GB/s theoretical bandwidth) and RTX 3090 (24 GB, 936 GB/s), both Ampere with CUDA 12.2. Default keys and payloads were 4-byte integers; the main narrow benchmark used 100% match and |S| = 2|R|. Their throughput covers join processing but excludes most allocations and auxiliary initialization; seven runs are summarized by the median. For narrow joins, their partitioned hash and sort-merge reached up to 4× cuDF's non-partitioned global hash throughput. This supports extra passes paying for locality in those workloads, not as a universal ranking. [Wu et al., *Efficiently Processing Large Relational Joins on GPUs* (arXiv v1)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00720v1)

The same paper separates wide-join materialization. With two payload columns on each side, PHJ-OM (partitioned hash with gather from transformed relations) was about 2.3× faster than PHJ-UM (unoptimized materialization) and 1.4× faster than SMJ-OM. Partitioning a column used two radix-partition invocations versus four sort-pairs invocations. But transformation is not free: below 25% match ratio, PHJ-UM was fastest because fewer scattered payloads were materialized; PHJ-UM also slightly beat PHJ-OM on smaller inputs. Optimizing materialization pays for large, high-match wide joins and can lose when few payloads are touched or inputs fit cache. With 8-byte values, extra sorting/partitioning narrowed SMJ-OM's benefit; PHJ-OM remained strongest in tested cases. At 2^27 rows per side, reported peak allocation was 12.6G for PHJ-UM versus 9.5G for PHJ-OM with 4-byte key/payload; for 8-byte key/payload it was 21.1G versus 18G. These allocations depend on that paper's workload and lifetime policy. [Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00720v1)

Rui and Tu's SSDBM 2017 CUDA implementation, compiled with NVCC 7.5, used equal-size relations with unique shuffled 32-bit keys and 32-bit payloads, one output per tuple, on a Maxwell Titan X (12 GB) and Kepler Titan (6 GB). Its hash join is radix-partitioned, not a direct global table. In their implementation, sort-merge was up to 26% faster than radix hash because sorting/merging used shared memory and registers more effectively while radix scatter had less locality. A prior hash implementation changed from two to three passes at 32M tuples and incurred a sudden runtime increase; their revised partitioner kept two passes over a broader range. The prior code could not run a 128M table on the 6 GB Titan. This is a pass-count and capacity cliff for a specific workload, not a general threshold. [Rui and Tu, *Fast Equi-Join Algorithms on GPUs*](https://cse.usf.edu/~tuy/pub/SSDBM17.pdf)

Earlier G80-era work exposes output and skew limits. He et al. tested an NVIDIA 8800 GTX with 768 MB device memory and 16 KB local memory per processor, using synthetic relations of 4-byte record IDs and keys; default hash and sort-merge inputs were 16M rows. Hash partitioned until most inner partitions fit local memory, then chunked oversized partitions with nested-loop fallback. Its output path counted matches, prefix-scanned exact offsets, then emitted; if output exceeded device memory, emission used multiple passes. This preserves multiplicity but shows why the worst-case |R|×|S| output cannot be preallocated. In the default uniform case, GPU hash took 1.3 s and sort-merge 2.0 s end-to-end. As one side's fraction of a constant key increased, their hash and sort-merge speedups against CPU stayed stable due to explicit skew handling; that does not make skew costless. [He et al., *Relational Joins on Graphics Processors*](https://cse.hkust.edu.hk/~psander/docs/join.pdf)

## Skew, load factor, and width

Track **key-frequency skew** separately from **partition-occupancy skew**. A few frequent values can concentrate build/probe work; partition occupancy is uneven rows per radix/hash partition and can overload blocks or local tables. For primary/foreign-key data, skewed foreign keys may reduce materialization when many reference missing values. In a many-to-many join, a frequent key on both sides instead multiplies output fanout. Equal work per partitioning thread does not eliminate hot-key output cost.

Wu et al. tested Zipf-skewed foreign keys with two payload columns and 2^27 rows per side on A100. Their bucket-chaining PHJ-UM degraded above Zipf factor 1 as bucket allocation/bookkeeping became imbalanced and synchronization-heavy. Radix PHJ-OM and SMJ were more stable; large PHJ partitions were subdivided and merge-path balanced SMJ matching. SMJ-UM became more competitive above Zipf 1 as skew reduced materialization work, while PHJ-OM stayed best in their tested range. This is primary-foreign-key evidence, not arbitrary many-to-many hot-key evidence. [Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00720v1)

Load factor controls direct-table capacity and probes: increasing α saves table space but can raise collision/probe work, especially for unsuccessful lookup. Awad et al.'s static GPU hash-table study used an NVIDIA TITAN V (Volta, 12 GB), CUDA 11.1, Xeon E5-2637, and 50M unique 32-bit keys. Its bucketed cuckoo table averaged 1.43 insertion probes at load factor 0.99 and 2.8 probes for negative lookup at 0.99. At load factor 0.8 it reported 1,425 Mkey/s insertion throughput. These are table microbenchmarks, not join results: they do not model duplicate lists, row payloads, output, or end-to-end allocation. A probe count alone cannot set a join crossover. [Awad et al., *Better GPU Hash Tables*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07232v3)

## Applicability and limits

Building on the smaller side usually reduces table capacity, but that side can still be a poor build choice if wide payloads, duplicate chains, or high α exceed memory. A large probe side amortizes partition setup; similarly sized inputs can favor either partitioned hash or sort depending on scatter locality, sorting implementation, and whether payload materialization is timed. Wider keys/payloads increase table slots and transformed/sort traffic. Narrow key-plus-ID matching can reduce transformation storage but make materialization dominate. Include output cardinality and requested output columns alongside |R|, |S|, and load factor.

These are not controlled cross-paper rankings. The G80 report includes device/host transfer in total time; Rui and Tu report total and kernel breakdown, with transfers about 35% of new sort-merge and 27% of new hash total; the 2023 work excludes most allocation/initialization. Key distributions, widths, outputs, GPUs, libraries, and time boundaries differ. The evidence supports separately measuring transform/build, probe/merge, output count/emission, payload materialization, peak live memory, and transfer/allocation. It does not identify a Strata/Arc workload or establish an implementation choice for it.
