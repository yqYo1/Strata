# When temporal blocking helps iterative GPU stencils, and when halo and resource costs erase the gain

**Assignment:** R876, knowledge collection. **Question:** For iterative single-GPU 3D stencils, when does temporal blocking save enough off-chip traffic to pay for redundant halo work, local storage, synchronization, and occupancy loss? This note compares a streaming baseline, spatial blocking, N.5D/register or shared-memory blocking, and deeper device-level blocking. It uses two original GPU stencil studies; it does not prescribe code changes for Strata.

## Short answer

Temporal blocking is most promising when the unblocked kernel is demonstrably limited by external-memory traffic, the domain is large enough to keep the device busy, the stencil has a compact halo relative to the useful tile, and a few time steps fit on chip without spilling or throttling the work in flight. The ideal benefit grows with blocked time depth because intermediate states need not be written to and reread from device memory. The practical benefit does not grow monotonically: halo volume grows with radius and time depth; sharing, indexing, barriers, and extra arithmetic consume throughput; larger register/shared-memory allocations reduce resident work; and deep schedules can cross over from bandwidth-bound to local-memory- or latency-bound.

There is no universal preferred depth. On Tesla V100, AN5D measured useful scaling to degree 5 for 3D star stencils but only degree 3 for 3D box stencils; its highest-order 3D box cases did not scale. EBISU's A100 results show a device-wide tile improving its 3D 7-point case while losing for its 2D case because barrier cost outweighed saved halo work. These outcomes are not portable crossover constants.

## Designs and cost ledger

| Design | Reuse / off-chip effect | Main added cost | Likely crossover |
|---|---|---|---|
| Streaming, one kernel per step | Neighbor reads may hit cache; each step still materializes its output and reads the next input | Kernel launch and full-grid intermediate traffic | Best reference; often wins for short runs, small domains, or compute-bound stencils |
| Spatial block, one step | Reuses neighbor values in registers/shared memory within a step; still stores each step | Halo loads, synchronization within a block, local storage | Useful when one-step reuse is missing from caches and tile occupancy remains healthy |
| N.5D/register or shared-memory temporal block | Keeps a slab/plane and several time levels local; amortizes global stores/loads over depth | Overlapped halos, extra local movement, registers/shared memory, more complex scheduling | Candidate for large domains, low/moderate radius and depth, and efficient local-memory hardware |
| Deep device tile or wavefront/diamond schedule | Can retain a larger region across more steps; geometric schedules can avoid redundant compute | Device-level coordination, reduced independent parallel tiles, storage capacity; geometry can hurt cache behavior | Candidate only when memory savings exceed whole-device synchronization or parallelism loss |

For a simple overlapped tile, let the stencil radius be `r`, blocked time depth `t`, and tile extent in each blocked dimension be `B_i` (including halo). The dependency halo grows by `r*t` on each side. A first-order useful-work fraction is

`eta ≈ Π_i max(0, 1 - 2*r*t/B_i)`, with redundant-work factor `1/eta`.

For a streamed dimension in an N.5D design, omit that dimension from the product; this is one reason N.5D can scale better than blocking every dimension at the same on-chip capacity. The model assumes regular interior tiles and an overlapped schedule; star versus box shape changes neighbor count and local traffic, while boundary tiles and uneven dimensions change the actual ratio. It is a geometric estimate, not a timing model.

If a scalar uses `s` bytes, an idealized tile with no intermediate global materialization needs about `2s/t` bytes per useful cell-step: one input and one final output amortized over `t` steps. The streaming lower bound is `2s`, though real traffic depends on cache and writes. This is an upper bound on benefit. A crossover model must compare measured global traffic saved against halo recomputation, local-memory traffic, synchronization, and occupancy or spill penalties; a bandwidth-only roofline misses these costs.

## What the GPU measurements show

AN5D evaluates single- and double-precision stencils on NVIDIA Tesla P100 and V100, including synthetic star and box shapes of radii 1–4. Its large domains are `16,384²` in 2D and `512³` in 3D, with 1,000 iterations; each case is repeated five times after warm-up, with reported average performance excluding PCIe transfer. The authors tune time depth and spatial tile shape, and sometimes constrain registers to 32, 64, or 96 per thread. This is evidence for steady-state GPU kernel behavior on large domains, not for short inputs or end-to-end application latency.

For V100, its measured depth sweep scales to time depth 10 for 2D, 5 for 3D star, and 3 for 3D box stencils. For first-order stencils, reported best depth ranges are 8–15 in 2D and 3–5 in 3D. Many 2D and 3D star cases, even fourth-order ones, benefit at depth 2 or above. The counterexample is high-order 3D box: register demand and the halo-to-tile ratio are too high for performance to continue scaling with temporal depth. The paper reports about 60% of V100 peak compute even without temporal blocking for these cases, showing that strong non-temporal performance can remain available when blocking is a poor fit. [AN5D, original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.01473)

Peak shared-memory bandwidth is insufficient as a predictor: the same AN5D kernels achieved less than half the measured shared-memory bandwidth on P100 as on V100, although benchmarked peak bandwidth differed by under 10%. Its model overpredicted useful depth on P100; reducing one 3D star case from depth 5 to 3 improved measured performance. Local-memory efficiency and resource allocation belong in the crossover estimate, not just nominal capacity and bandwidth.

EBISU revisits time blocking on an NVIDIA A100-PCIe, using 2D and 3D stencil cases with large domains, one kernel run per measurement, warm-up, ten GPU-event repetitions, and the peak result. Its 3D data include a 7-point stencil over `2560×288×384`; its benchmark table also covers 13-, 17-, and 27-point stencils and Poisson. The paper reports a 3D 7-point result of 151 GCells/s without device-level tiling and 197 GCells/s with it (about 30% higher). In its 2D 5-point case, however, shared-memory overlapped tiling had about 95% valid work at depth 7. Device tiling could reach depth 15, but its modeled 1.2 μs grid synchronization against a 2.05 μs stencil time left only about 63% of runtime doing stencil work; the paper concludes that device tiling lost for the 2D cases it studied. [EBISU, original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07390)

The 3D EBISU case illustrates a different balance: an overlapped shared-memory design at depth 3 had a reported useful fraction around 77% and measured practical performance around 151 GCells/s, whereas deeper device tiling at depth 8 modeled around 67% useful runtime but reached 197 GCells/s measured. The paper's printed 3D valid-fraction expression `(34 - 2*r*t)^2 / 34^2` evaluates to about 68%, not its stated 77%, for `r=1,t=3`; this arithmetic inconsistency makes that fraction unsafe as an exact quantitative comparison. The measured 151/197 GCells/s comparison is separate evidence, but it still reflects the paper's A100 kernels, synchronization design, tile choices, and peak-of-ten timing.

## Practical decision rules and limits

1. **Establish the baseline bottleneck.** If memory traffic is not limiting, fewer global bytes can have little value. Compare full time-step sequences and include launch, boundary, and synchronization costs.
2. **Sweep radius, tile width, and time depth together.** Larger tiles lower halo fraction but consume local capacity and can reduce occupancy; deeper blocks save intermediate traffic but widen the halo and increase local operations. Use the valid-work model to prune poor tiles, then benchmark nearby feasible shapes and depths.
3. **Compare simpler alternatives.** Start from the streaming baseline, then test one-step spatial reuse, then a modest temporal block. Deep device tiling is a distinct option rather than the automatic next step: it can win for 3D when overlapped halo recomputation is costly, yet lose when device synchronization dominates. Nonredundant wavefront/diamond schedules avoid recomputation but introduce dependency ordering and may reduce block size or cache efficiency.
4. **Treat resource cliffs as crossover points.** Record registers per thread, local-memory allocation, spill traffic, achieved occupancy, and the measured shared/local and global bandwidth. The EBISU results suggest low occupancy can be deliberate when enough parallelism remains to saturate the device, but its design is not evidence that low occupancy is generally safe.
5. **Test realistic domains and boundaries.** AN5D chose domains large enough to stabilize GPU throughput; EBISU evaluates single deep kernels and reports peak samples. Neither establishes a benefit for small domains, few total steps, frequent boundary exchange, or launch-heavy applications. Too few independent tiles can leave the device idle despite a favorable per-tile traffic model.

The transferable lesson is to model and measure a whole dependency path using the target GPU's effective bandwidths, synchronization, occupancy, and spill behavior. The papers are CUDA/NVIDIA studies: EBISU explicitly uses CUDA grid synchronization and A100-specific copy/resource assumptions; AN5D uses CUDA-generated kernels and NVIDIA SM limits. Those names and values do not predict SYCL or Intel GPU behavior. A SYCL implementation must map the abstract choices to its actual subgroup/work-group model, shared local memory, barrier costs, global coordination mechanism, compiler register allocation, and device occupancy; kernel launch boundaries may replace a CUDA cooperative grid barrier but have different cost and scheduling semantics. No specific Intel crossover can be inferred from these measurements.

## Sources and evidence boundary

Two public original research papers were read in full text: Matsumura et al., *AN5D: Automated Stencil Framework for High-Degree Temporal Blocking on GPUs* (2020), and Zhang et al., *Revisiting Temporal Blocking Stencil Optimizations* (ICS 2023). Both report measured NVIDIA performance and explicit resource/time-depth effects. The halo equation above is a simplified derivation from overlapped-tile geometry; the byte lower bound is an idealized traffic model. Reported measured numbers are distinguished from those models. No practitioner blog was needed to resolve this question, and no local code, GPU, profiler, or product evidence was used.
