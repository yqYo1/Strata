# R863 — GPU FFT decomposition across on-chip and global-memory regimes

**Classification:** knowledge collection. **Scope:** public-source comparison of single-precision batched 1-D and 2-D complex FFT dataflows for power-of-two and smooth composite lengths. This is not a proposal to add FFTs to Strata or a prediction for Intel Arc.

## Findings in brief

A GPU FFT is a communication schedule around butterflies. A workgroup-local transform minimizes global traffic but consumes local storage and may underfill at low batch. Multi-pass Stockham is regular and naturally orders output, at the cost of a launch and full-array read/write per stage. Four-/six-step decomposition keeps sub-FFTs local but adds twiddle and transpose movement; its value depends on batch, local capacity, coalescing and whether a consumer accepts transposed output.

On Apple M1, Bergach reports a local radix-8 win at `N=4096`, batch 256, complex FP32: 138.45 GFLOP/s and 1.78 μs/transform versus radix-4's 113.6 GFLOP/s and 2.16 μs, and vDSP's 107 GFLOP/s and 2.29 μs. At this N, the paper says vDSP leads at batch ≤16 because of lower dispatch overhead, while the GPU exceeds it at batch ≥64. These are device-specific results; the paper's GPU command-buffer timing description does not clearly explain timing for CPU-side vDSP.

## Algorithmic dataflow and cost model

Let `N` be complex elements, `e=8` bytes per element for interleaved complex FP32, and `R` a radix. The counts below are algorithmic estimates, not measured traffic. They omit cache hits, twiddle table reads, launch overhead, padding, allocator effects and any final consumer that can accept a nonstandard order.

| Dataflow | Device-memory passes and scratch | Reuse, twiddles and synchronization | Where it tends to fit; counterevidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One-workgroup fused radix/Stockham** | About `2eN` global bytes (input plus output); intermediate stages stay local. Local storage is roughly `eN` to `2eN`, plus registers/padding. | Reuses data across `log_R(N)` stages; exchanges/barriers replace global passes. Stockham folds permutation into stage addresses. Higher radix reduces stages but raises live values and register use. | Fits when local state preserves occupancy and batch supplies enough workgroups; low batch exposes dispatch latency. |
| **Global multi-pass Stockham** | Each radix stage reads/writes the full array: approximately `2eN·log_R(N)` bytes and one `eN` ping-pong buffer. Each launch provides a global stage boundary. | Regular accesses and natural-order output avoid separate bit reversal; twiddles are computed or loaded per stage. | Simple, broad butterfly parallelism can win when local resource pressure dominates; repeated global traffic loses when bandwidth is scarce. |
| **Four-step/six-step or transpose-based** | Factor `N=N₁N₂`, run local sub-FFTs, twiddle and transpose/reorder. Each full-array pass costs about `2eN` bytes; scratch is often `eN` unless in-place transpose or layout reuse is possible. Pass count depends on factorization and output order. | Transpose makes strided sub-FFTs contiguous; FFT, twiddle and transpose may be fused. Batching sub-FFTs improves coalescing but consumes local storage/registers. | Useful when the transform cannot fit locally or strided access wastes transactions; loses when transpose traffic or a required reorder dominates. |

For row-major 2-D input, row FFTs are contiguous and column FFTs strided. Transposing between axes makes the latter contiguous; a consumer may absorb the final transpose if it accepts that layout. One explicit complex-FP32 transpose costs at least `16NₓNᵧ` bytes. Its value depends on the saved strided transactions and on whether one multi-axis plan or two 1-D plans better amortize setup and launches.

A compact model is `T ≈ T_setup + L·T_launch + B_global/BW_eff + F/F_eff + T_local + T_twiddle + T_order`. Local fusion wins when saved passes/launches exceed occupancy loss and there are enough workgroups. A transpose path wins when coalescing or smaller local sub-FFTs repay its bytes and scratch; it loses when transpose/reorder exceeds saved strided traffic. High radix helps only while fewer stages repay register, bank-conflict and residency costs.

Batching affects both occupancy and access shape. Many short transforms provide independent workgroups and let lanes load adjacent values; one short transform may leave most compute units idle. Assigning several transforms to one group can improve coalescing, but grows its register and local-memory footprint. Splitting a large sequence into sub-FFTs exposes parallel work but adds twiddle and reorder traffic. Record batch and local grouping with `N`; size alone does not predict the winner.

## Published results and crossover evidence

Bergach (arXiv:2603.27569v1, submitted 2026-03-29) reports the M1 results above for `N=4096`, batch 256, complex FP32, with median GPU times after warmup. Radix-8 uses 512 threads and about 38 GPR/thread; its four stages beat radix-4's six by 22%. The authors place an occupancy cliff near 128 GPR/thread; radix-16 would use 61% before temporaries, and radix-32 spills. Radix-16 was not directly timed. `N=8192` and `16384` require four-step decomposition and an intermediate device-memory transpose because they exceed the 32 KiB local limit. Their multi-size table lists 112 and 103 GFLOP/s but omits exact “optimal” batch values, so these are not same-batch transpose comparisons.

At `N=4096`, batch ≤16 favors Apple's vDSP reference; GPU performance exceeds it at batch ≥64. This shows a local kernel can lose despite avoiding intermediate global traffic when dispatch cost is not amortized or the eight-core GPU is underfilled. The paper gives no numerical error bound against vDSP.

The paper identifies an 8-core, 1278 MHz Apple M1 GPU with 68 GB/s unified-memory bandwidth running macOS. It uses 100 warmups then 1,000 measured iterations and reports median Metal command-buffer timestamps, but names no OS or Metal version. These are steady-state execution results; shader compilation and first-call setup are outside the measured loop. The CPU vDSP timing boundary remains unclear.

Govindaraju et al. (SC 2008 revision, GTX280/8800) test `float2` complex FP32. GPU time is the minimum over repeats; library configuration and host-device copies are excluded. In batched 1-D tests, Figure 12 fixes `E=2²³`, `M=E/N`: `N=256` has 32,768 transforms; `N=8192` has 1,024. The authors report up to 4× cuFFT for large N but do not identify the maximizing point. For `N>2¹⁰`, the hierarchical/shared-memory curve drops as N grows while the global-memory path stays nearly constant; they tune the crossover at runtime. This is evidence that a transpose/hierarchical path can lose on the tested hardware, not a modern-GPU prediction.

The study names cuFFT 1.1 but only describes its GPU drivers as recent, so the ratios are historical and cannot identify a modern library's plan. Its 2-D batch series fixes total elements rather than batch; smaller transforms therefore have more independent images. The changing batch alters parallelism as well as transform size.

Figure 14 tests square 2-D transforms and a batch series with `E=2²⁴`; `256×256` therefore has batch 256. Their hierarchical kernel fuses sub-FFT, twiddle and transpose. The paper reports up to 3× cuFFT for large *single* 2-D transforms, but omits cuFFT in the batched panel because version 1.1 lacked batched 2-D plans; its maximum is not tied to one N. This supports transpose-fused wins in some cases, not a matched comparison at `256×256` or across all three algorithms.

## Library contracts, correctness and limits

Vendor APIs expose contracts, not a guaranteed algorithm. cuFFT 13.4 supports batched/layout plans, work-area queries and multiple internal kernels; C2C forward/inverse is unnormalized, so a round trip scales by the element count. rocFFT 1.0.40 plans encode dimensions, batch, precision, placement, layout and scale; interleaved complex stores real/imaginary pairs, and a required work buffer must be supplied or is allocated. oneMKL 2026.0 maps real forward input to conjugate-even output, of which only part is stored. Comparisons must fix C2C/R2C, precision, placement, strides, output order and scaling.

Radix and twiddle changes alter FP32 rounding; compare against a reference with tolerance, correct inverse scaling and Hermitian checks for real transforms. A transpose changes physical order even if values are mathematically equivalent; a layout-aware consumer can avoid a final reorder. Published data do not predict other GPUs or Intel Arc. Bergach's arXiv v1 is CC BY 4.0 and states its implementation is MIT-licensed; no code was inspected or copied. Govindaraju et al.'s IEEE paper is paraphrased only.

## One bounded future experiment design (not run)

On one GPU compare the three dataflows for 1-D `N=4096, 8192, 16384` and 2-D `256×256, 1024×1024`, batches 16/64/256/1,024. Require natural-order, unnormalized C2C FP32 output and identical placement; report a transposed-layout consumer separately. Record plan time, per-run device and host times, launches, bytes, peak scratch, local resource use and reference error. This separates transpose and stage traffic from launch/setup; it is a design only.
