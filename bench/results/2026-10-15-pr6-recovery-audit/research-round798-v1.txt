# R798 — Geometry-dependent scheduling for dense and ragged matrix work

**Purpose:** knowledge_archive (knowledge collection for a future concrete scheduling question). **Retrieved:** 2026-10-16. **Question:** Across skinny, small variable-size batches and large dense 16-bit GEMMs, what source-backed mechanisms govern output-tile ownership, operand layout/reuse, padding versus grouped scheduling, and crossover? What can be transferred as a hypothesis to Intel Xe2/SYCL? No claim here identifies a Strata bottleneck or predicts a local speed result.

## Scope and evidence boundary

This GEMM work-partitioning scope is distinct from R783/R793’s dtype and library API contracts, R789’s pointwise residual mapping/fusion, R566’s MoE GEMM discussion, and R763’s general residency/pipeline scope. The index describes R778 as sparse SpMV/SpMM and R779 as irregular gather; neither addresses dense GEMM tile scheduling. R628 concerns a GEMM scheduling census and R701 the local grouped-GEMM/prefill route. Their bodies are not reopened here, and no local shape distribution is inferred. This report compares external mechanisms only.

Baseline: one work unit owns a BM×BN tile of C and accumulates K from BM×BK and BK×BN operands. This single writer keeps partial sums local, while the number and shape of C tiles determine available parallel work. Neighboring tiles may reload reusable A or B values. Larger BM/BN can improve reuse but enlarge accumulators and staged operands, reducing resident groups through register or shared-local-memory pressure. Orientation depends on contiguous dimensions and tile traversal. Transposes or changed strides can spoil coalescing; store layouts may require conversion or shared-memory exchange. Global, staging, subgroup and output layouts must therefore be considered together.

Abdelfattah et al.’s MAGMA study uses FP16 Tensor Core tiles on Tesla V100; its WMMA interface permits FP16 or FP32 C/D, but the cited discussion does not establish the experiment’s accumulator, so HGEMM is not enough to infer it. A CTA owns C across K; larger blocks improve A/B reuse. Yet excess warps can reduce occupancy: with 64-sized blocks and 16-sized Tensor Core subtiles, their 32-warp CTA leaves half the warps without compute work. Partial blocks are zero-padded in shared staging, showing padding and large tiles have costs.

## Shape-dependent cases

| Shape regime | Likely strength of uniform padding / one schedule | Likely strength of per-shape or grouped work | Crossover risks to measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tiny M,N; many independent GEMMs | Simple fixed tile avoids descriptor or queue work | Place multiple small GEMMs in one matrix operation or assign several tiles per group to fill matrix lanes and hide latency | Padding wastes inactive lanes; packing needs compatible dimensions/layout and enough batch count; scalar or SIMD compute can beat a matrix engine when useful work per instruction is very low |
| Ragged small/medium M,N,K batch | A uniform upper-bound tile has easy dispatch but may launch empty work and overcompute edge tiles | Choose tiles per GEMM and schedule tile descriptors together; distribute small-K tiles to expose more instruction-level work | Descriptor/indexing, sorting, divergent tile work and reduced locality can exceed saved inactive work; grouping may underfill a matrix instruction when shapes differ too much |
| Tall/skinny, especially small N or K | Padding to square/large matrix-engine tiles can burn most operations on empty rows/columns | Orient the mapping around the long dimension, use lanes across independent rows, or group compatible skinny products | Skinny orientation can lower reuse of one operand; smaller tiles can increase launch/tile overhead or fail to hide latency; many tiny output tiles can be bandwidth/latency-bound |
| Large dense M,N,K with many full output tiles | One owner per C tile preserves reuse, local accumulation, and aligned waves | Grouped scheduling is useful only for remaining heterogeneous/tail work or when tile quantization leaves large idle capacity | Splitting K or work-centric scheduling creates partial-C storage/reduction and sync; smaller tiles raise memory traffic and may lose matrix-engine efficiency |

Li et al.’s coordinated tiling/batching study selects tiles per differently shaped GEMM, then assigns one or more tiles per block. Larger tiles improve reuse but reduce TLP; small K can leave a block underfilled, while bundling tiles along K can improve ILP. In its V100 FP32 GoogleNet test, only layers with small M/K visibly benefit from batching. The transferable point is the interaction of tile choice and group depth, not its FP32 result or thresholds.

For tall-and-skinny GEMM, Rivera et al.’s TSM2X (v5, 2021) distinguishes m≈k≫n from m≫k≈n. Its CUDA kernels cover FP32/FP64 on K40c, M40, P100 and V100, not FP16. A row-per-thread mapping coalesces tall-A access, keeps output vectors in registers, and stages narrow B in shared memory. Wider N improves reuse; very small N tends toward memory limits, while small K with long M can be latency-bound. This cautions against universal matrix-tile padding, but its SIMT results do not establish XMX behavior.

Osama et al.’s Stream-K study covers 32,824 NVIDIA A100 shapes with FP16 inputs and FP32 accumulation/output. Regular output tiles work well with enough waves; poor tile-count quantization leaves a small tail of long-running tiles. Stream-K balances K-loop work, but CTAs crossing output tiles need scratch, synchronization and partial-C collection, with cache costs; the authors hybridize it with regular tiles. They report weak results for small bandwidth-bound problems, where partial traffic is not repaid. Work balancing matters most when tile quantization consumes substantial runtime, less when full waves and reuse dominate.

For a batch, useful-MAC fraction is Σ(MᵢNᵢKᵢ)/Σ(MpadᵢNpadᵢKpadᵢ). It measures wasted work, not runtime. Padding may win through a regular kernel that avoids metadata, scheduling or launches. Grouping may win when inactive work is large, enough products fill groups, and operand access stays contiguous. Uneven K loops, misaligned subtiles, descriptor/reduction costs or little cross-product reuse can erase utilization gains. Separate products share no operands unless data actually overlap; output ownership remains per product.

## Intel Xe2/SYCL transfer assumptions

CUTLASS’s efficient-GEMM guide describes CTA/warp/thread tiles, shared-memory and register staging, a separate epilogue for coalesced stores, and split-K reduction when CTA count is low. This CUDA guide is not a source port. Intel oneAPI 2025.2 documents subgroup-collective `joint_matrix` and hardware-dependent XMX tile combinations, with no fallback/emulation. NVIDIA tile sizes, warp counts, thresholds, bank behavior and speedups do not transfer numerically to Xe2. SYCL questions include fragment ownership, legal device/runtime combinations, staging/register occupancy, stride coalescing and descriptor or reduction cost. These remain hypotheses; no source establishes an Xe2 optimum or a Strata bottleneck.

## Bounded discrimination experiment (prospective only)

Once an owned BF16/FP16 fixture and correctness contract exist, compare three policies on one Xe2: (1) regular output tiles with masked edges; (2) padding to supported XMX tiles; (3) grouping tile descriptors by a small shape bucket, one owner per output tile and no K split. Use one skinny, ragged-batch and large-dense case with stated accumulation/output mode. Hold values, strides, useful MACs and batch identity fixed; vary policy only. Validate against FP32, recording padding fraction, edge tiles, descriptors, estimated SLM/register use and workgroup count. Measure the whole sequence in owned clean processes, including packing, descriptors and extra kernels; retain samples, ordering, toolchain/device and faults under artifact policy. Do not sweep tile sizes. Test whether overhead tracks inactive work or instead active groups, descriptors, traffic, occupancy or launch/reduction costs. Results discriminate only these cases, not a general schedule or model performance.
