# Fusion and materialization boundaries in GPU analytical query pipelines

**Purpose:** knowledge collection. This is a literature comparison, not a Strata implementation or performance claim.

## Question and comparison boundary

For a columnar relation with `n` rows, a selection predicate that keeps fraction `s`, a projected payload of `w` bytes per surviving row, and an aggregate, when should a GPU query engine retain operator boundaries, fuse the whole local pipeline, or insert selected staging points? The evidence below covers relational scans, selection/compaction, projection, simple aggregation, and pipeline breakers. It does not compare hash-join algorithms or import data-path IO/decompression results.

| Execution style | Work between global-memory boundaries | Best case | Main costs or boundaries | Evidence and timing boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Operator-at-a-time | Each selection, projection, and aggregate is a separate operator/kernel; rows or indices are materialized between them. | A narrow intermediate is cheap to store, or multiple consumers can reuse it. | Per-stage launches, synchronization, writes/reads or index-plus-gather traffic, and allocation. | In SSB Q21 at SF16 on an A100, warm TQP used 190 kernels; `idx_select` took 9.55 ms (23.13% of GPU time). This is not a controlled fusion-only comparison. |
| Fully fused local pipeline | One kernel evaluates predicates, computes projected values, and feeds a local or partial aggregate without writing an intermediate relation. | A single-consumer chain avoids wide temporaries and repeated compaction; launch reduction matters for short stages. | Selection still scans predicate columns and compacts returned rows; aggregation may need partials/finalization. More live values can raise register/shared-memory demand. | On a Tesla C2070, two chained 50%-selectivity filters over random 32-bit values (25% survive) had 6.2% higher throughput than keeping the first output in GPU memory, and 49.9% higher than round-tripping it through the host. The latter includes PCIe. |
| Staged or relaxed fusion | Fuse row-local operators within a stage; materialize IDs or values at a global dependency, reuse point, or resource boundary. | Keeps simple chains local while preserving independent work for a sort/hash build or high-fan-out result. | Boundaries add launches and traffic; oversized stages can increase live state or duplicate branch work. | Crystal and HeavyDB fused most of SSB Q21 within four kernels but separated hash-join build from dependent work. A CPU compiler study gives a staged analogy, not GPU performance evidence. |

## A bounded traffic and launch model

Let `b` be the bytes per input row needed for predicate evaluation, `s` the survivor fraction, and `w` the bytes per projected survivor. A materialized projected relation costs about `n*s*w` bytes written and then about the same read by its next consumer: roughly `2*n*s*w` bytes of intermediate traffic. If the engine materializes only a 32-bit row ID, the write/read payload is nearer `8*n*s` bytes, but the consumer still gathers its needed columns. A validity bitmap is smaller again (about `n/8` bytes per pass), but it still needs a later pass or gather. These are byte counts, not paper timing claims; they omit alignment, cache hits, allocation, and compaction scratch.

A useful first-order comparison is

`T ≈ K*L + bytes/B_eff + work/C_eff + sync + setup`,

where `K` is the launch count, `L` launch cost, `B_eff` and `C_eff` the achieved bandwidth and compute rate, and `sync` includes prefix/offset coordination and stage barriers. Fusion wins only when removed launches, intermediate traffic, duplicate scans, and compactions outweigh slower execution from resource pressure, less parallelism, or repeated work. No cited experiment reports a portable per-launch constant, so estimating `L` from one device and applying it to another would be unsound.

### What the measurements show

The clearest controlled win is Wu et al.'s 2012 kernel fusion/fission study. Its CUDA environment was a Tesla C2070 (6 GB), dual Xeon E5520 host, Ubuntu 10.04, and NVCC 4.0 (paper §II, Table II, p. 2). The selection microbenchmark uses random 32-bit integers; two filters each keep 50%, so 25% survive. Fused throughput averaged 49.9% above the host-round-trip approach and 6.2% above retaining the first result in GPU memory. The round-trip was 54.0% of that method's total time. The paper separates total-throughput results from GPU-compute-only breakdowns and tests 4.19 million to 415.24 million elements (§III.B, pp. 4–5); it reports no compilation/setup time. A three-filter sweep reports 2.35x the throughput of three unfused selections, versus 1.80x for two fused selections. These are manually transformed Fermi kernels, not current-device predictions.

Selection is not free to fuse away. The same implementation partitions input, filters within CTAs, buffers survivors, then gathers them after global synchronization (§II, pp. 1–2). Fusion shares partition/buffer/gather work, but a returned relation still needs correct output positions. The sensitivity test finds larger gains when more rows survive, because more intermediate data would otherwise move; fewer survivors also mean less output traffic in both methods. Survivor count alone does not decide the boundary.

For width and early projection, Red Fox's TPC-H Q1 example is instructive. Moving selection earlier and aggregating as soon as inputs were ready cut its intermediate footprint in half; execution excluding PCIe fell to 0.16 seconds, reported as 1.8x faster (CGO 2014, §5.3, p. 9). The test uses TPC-H SF1 on a GeForce Titan; it gives no separate compile/setup timing. This is plan ordering and early discard, not a fusion ablation. It supports the deduction that reducing `w` lowers boundary cost even without changing fusion.

Cao et al.'s 2023 characterization makes the materialization penalty visible in a newer stack. It uses an A100 40 GB and SSB at SF16 for most experiments; warm timings assume data resident in GPU memory and query parse/optimization/compilation already complete (§3.1–3.4, pp. 3–4). For Q21, BlazingSQL and TQP launch 172 and 190 kernels, versus four in Crystal and HeavyDB. In TQP's kernel breakdown, `idx_select` constructs a new tensor after predicate evaluation and consumes 9.55 ms, or 23.13% of GPU compute; the paper attributes this to intermediate creation in the library-backed plan (§5.1.2, p. 6). The systems use different execution models and algorithms, so these figures demonstrate an opportunity and a failure mode, not a controlled causal speedup for fusion. Their cold-execution analysis separately finds transfer, optimization, compilation, and setup overhead larger than compute for the studied systems; warm kernel timings therefore do not predict first-query latency.

## Where full fusion loses or stops

1. **Resource pressure:** Wu et al. explicitly warn that fusing too many operators raises per-thread register and shared-memory pressure, potentially adding spills or adverse cache effects (§III.C, p. 5). The paper does not measure a crossover point, so this is a documented mechanism, not a quantified regression threshold. Red Fox likewise notes that intermediate tuple size competes for register/shared-memory capacity needed for occupancy (§5.3, p. 9). Wide projections and a long live range make this risk more plausible.
2. **A true pipeline breaker:** TPC-H Q1 in the 2012 study has a sort between fused join/filter work and the later arithmetic/aggregation; the sort waits for its whole input and cannot fuse with the neighboring stages. Sort is about 71% of baseline execution, so fusing the eligible work gives 1.25x overall; adding fission gives 26.5% total improvement, while the remaining fusible block is reported at 3.18x. Q21 sees 13.2% overall improvement, versus 1.22x over its fusible block (§V, pp. 7–8). These examples show why a fast local fused region may have a modest whole-query effect. Cao et al. similarly identify hash-join build as Q21's breaker: the fused systems keep that phase separate, while fusing as much as possible around it.
3. **Reuse and branch fan-out:** the traffic model predicts that one materialization can beat recomputing the same predicate/projection independently for several consumers. This is a workload-dependent deduction rather than a measured result in the cited GPU papers. It should be assessed against the bytes written/read, consumer count, and whether each branch can be fused with useful downstream work.

The CPU-only relaxed-operator-fusion study by Menon et al. provides a useful compiler analogy: it inserts stage boundaries to restore vector batches and prefetch opportunity, and argues that neither fully vectorized nor fully pipelined execution is universally best (§4, pp. 4–5). Its SIMD occupancy and cache results cannot be transferred to GPU kernels; the relevant transferable point is only that a stage can deliberately buy parallel work or better memory scheduling at the cost of materialization.

## Evidence limits and novelty

These papers differ in GPU generation, layout, workload, and timing boundary. C2070 selection tests use synthetic 32-bit columns and report PCIe-inclusive throughput separately from GPU compute. Red Fox uses TPC-H SF1 on a GeForce Titan and excludes PCIe for its plan-ordering result. Cao et al. report warm SSB SF16 on an A100 across different systems and layouts. None gives a universal threshold or validates a Strata/Arc/SYCL workload. This collection focuses on relational selection/projection/aggregation boundaries, excluding tensor, graph, and convolution results.

The transformations were manual and the kernels were not further hand-optimized.
- [Cao et al., “GPU Database Systems Characterization and Optimization” (PVLDB 17(3), 2023)](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p441-cao.pdf): A100/SSB/workload and warm-versus-cold definitions in §3, pp. 3–4; kernel counts and Q21 timing in §5.1.2, p. 6. The SF16 runs are in-memory; the cross-system comparison does not isolate fusion as one variable.
- [Wu et al., “Red Fox: An Execution Environment for Relational Query Processing on GPUs” (CGO 2014)](https://istc-cc.cmu.edu/publications/papers/2014/cgo14_redfox.pdf): TPC-H system and execution context in §5, pp. 7–9; Q1 plan-ordering and half-sized intermediate in §5.3, p. 9. The 1.8x timing excludes PCIe and is not a kernel-fusion ablation.
- [Menon et al., “Relaxed Operator Fusion for In-Memory Databases” (PVLDB 11(1), 2017)](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p1-menon.pdf): motivation and stage model in §4, pp. 4–5; SIMD compaction example in §4.2, p. 5. This is CPU evidence only and is cited as a scheduling analogy, not GPU performance evidence.
