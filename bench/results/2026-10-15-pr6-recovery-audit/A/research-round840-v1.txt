# R840 — Mixed-precision iterative refinement in GPU dense solvers

**Purpose:** knowledge collection. **Retrieved:** 2026-10-16 JST. **Question:** what do two implemented dense GPU solver designs actually store, recompute, scale, and test when low-precision factorization is paired with higher-precision residual/correction? No Strata inference proposal or Xe2 prediction follows.

## Scope and increment

I checked `AGENTS.md`, `docs/ARTIFACT_RETENTION.md`, the research index, and the relevant R778/R825 material. R778 concerns sparse storage conversion and format reuse, not a feedback solver. R825 concerns adversarial GEMM arithmetic and output rounding; its high-precision dot oracle is not an iterative solve acceptance criterion. R840 adds the missing linear-system loop: retain a matrix, recompute its residual, apply a low-precision factor/preconditioner, update the solution, and accept against a backward-error bound. This also differs from recurrent-state checkpoint dtype work: it is an algorithm with a fixed operator and convergence test.

The two implementations are NVIDIA cuSOLVER’s `cusolverDnIRSXgesv` public interface, documented in CUDA/cuSOLVER 13.4, and MAGMA’s `magma_dhgesv_iteref_gpu` FP64–FP16-Tensor-Core path, from MAGMA v2.5.0 at commit `630c968a10c4d67a6e679ba942561520160bfc5d`. cuSOLVER is closed source, so 13.4 is the exact public API/documentation revision, not an asserted kernel source commit. The cited 2020 MAGMA measurements identify their machine and CUDA 10.1 but do not disclose a source commit; do not combine those measurements with a claim about bit-identical behavior of the pinned v2.5.0 code. [cuSOLVER 13.4 IRS documentation](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cusolver/); [MAGMA official releases](https://icl.utk.edu/magma/downloads/)

## Design contracts compared

| Contract | cuSOLVER IRS `Xgesv` | MAGMA `dhgesv_iteref_gpu` |
|---|---|---|
| Problem and precision | General dense LU solve. Main input/output precision may be FP64 while lowest factorization precision is FP16, BF16 or TF32 subject to device support. | General dense LU with FP64 `A`, `B`, and `X`; FP16 Tensor-Core path is selected for the factorization, with FP64 output. The implementation’s update path uses FP16 operands with FP32 accumulation; it is not a persistent all-FP16 factor array. |
| User-visible and materialized buffers | FP64/main-precision `dA`, `dB`, `dX`, pivots/status, and device workspace sized by `IRSXgesv_bufferSize` after setting parameters. On convergence `dA` is unchanged; on enabled fallback it may contain the main-precision LU factors. | `dA` FP64 input, `dB` FP64 RHS, `dX` FP64 solution, host/device pivots, caller `dworkd` FP64 residual of `n*nrhs`, and caller `dworks` FP32 storage documented as `n*(n+nrhs)`. In v2.5.0 source, `dworks` aliases the solve scratch `dSX`; the persistent padded FP32 factor copy is separately allocated as `dA_sprec`. On successful IR `dA` is unchanged; fallback leaves FP64 factors there. |
| Correction choices/cadence | Classical IR, GMRES-IR, classical outer IR with inner GMRES, or GMRES-GMRES. Total default iteration budget is 50; inner tolerance defaults to `1e-4` for nested choices. The GMRES variants are limited to one RHS. | The v2.5.0 `dhgesv_iteref_gpu` wrapper selects full preconditioned GMRES (`Magma_REFINE_GMSTRS`): one FP64 Krylov solve applies original `A` and uses the mixed LU factors as preconditioner. The expert interface also offers classical IR and IRGMRES, which recomputes residuals at outer steps. Its GMRES iteration cap is `min(n,500)`; nested inner solves default to `1e-4`. |
| Scaling and fallback | Public IRS contract specifies no automatic equilibration. It detects unwise low-precision choice, conversion overflow, factorization/computation errors, and nonconvergence through status/iteration outputs. Fallback to main precision is enabled by default and refactors from scratch. | The general mixed-precision study used two-sided diagonal scaling for its measured `dhgesv-TC` table. The broader MAGMA design also uses scalar scaling to avoid FP16 range problems; diagonal shifts apply to its SPD Cholesky variant, not general LU. Fallback refactors in FP64 on low-precision failure, overflow, or iteration exhaustion. |
| Acceptance | Default residual test is `||r||∞ < sqrt(n) ||x||∞ ||A||∞ eps_main BWDMAX`, with `BWDMAX=1`; tolerance is configurable. `niters<0` signals an unsuccessful mixed path, including “not worth low precision,” narrowing overflow, factorization failure, compute overflow, or max iterations. | MAGMA’s API documentation states the normwise backward-error threshold `||r||∞ < sqrt(n)||x||∞||A||∞eps64` (`BWDMAX=1`). In the pinned full-GMRES path, source sets a GMRES tolerance from `||A||∞ eps64 sqrt(n)` and caps iterations at `min(n,500)`; negative `iter` falls back to FP64. The published study separately reports its acceptance as relative residual `< eps64 sqrt(n)`. |

The MAGMA API reference names its caller arrays and nominal extents, but the pinned source resolves actual lifetimes: it converts original `dA` to an internally allocated `dA_sprec` with leading dimension rounded to 32, keeps original FP64 `dA` for residuals, and aliases external `dworkd` to residual `dR`. In the `Magma_PREC_SHT` branch, factorization selects a mixed GEMM mode with 16-bit inputs and 32-bit output/accumulation; factors persist in FP32. The GPU FGMRES helper separately allocates FP64 `V` and `W` arrays for its restart basis. cuSOLVER instead exposes one opaque workspace byte count; factor arrays, GMRES basis storage, conversion staging, and per-iteration scratch are not itemized. It optionally retains outer residual history in a `(max_iters+1) × 2` table, with FP64 norms for FP64 input. Do not infer internal sizes from the workspace query alone. [MAGMA dense GESV API](https://icl.utk.edu/projectsfiles/magma/doxygen/group__magma__gesv.html); [pinned MAGMA v2.5.0 factorization interface](https://github.com/icl-utk-edu/magma/blob/630c968a10c4d67a6e679ba942561520160bfc5d/src/dxgesv_gmres_gpu.cpp); [pinned MAGMA v2.5.0 FGMRES buffers](https://github.com/icl-utk-edu/magma/blob/630c968a10c4d67a6e679ba942561520160bfc5d/src/dgmres_plu_gpu.cpp)

## What is materialized and what is recomputed

For MAGMA v2.5.0, the source allocates `dA_sprec` as `roundup(n,32)*n` floats, converts the FP64 coefficient matrix once, and keeps that FP32 factor array for preconditioning; FP64 `dA` remains for high-precision products. At one RHS, that persistent internal matrix is about `4 n²` bytes and the caller’s residual vector is `8 n` bytes. At `n=16,830`, `dA_sprec` is about 1.06 GiB (decimal allocation arithmetic: 1.13 GB), versus 2.11 GiB for FP64 `dA`; the separately required `dworks` caller allocation must not be counted as another factor copy because this implementation aliases it to `dSX` solve scratch. For the expert GMSTRS FGMRES path, source sizes FP64 basis/work arrays as `(m+1)*n2` and `m*n2` doubles, with `n2=roundup(n,64)` and maximum `m=500`; the maximum pair is about 128 MiB at this matrix order. That is a source-derived capacity, not a measured allocation receipt for the paper’s run. FP16-TC arithmetic reduces update work inside GEMM, but no persistent full-half factor appears in this path.

Each new Krylov basis step reads original `A` for a high-precision matrix-vector product and applies the FP32 LU preconditioner; the full-GMRES solve computes a residual as it begins and FGMRES holds its basis/work vectors across steps. That avoids rematerializing the current Krylov basis at each product at the cost of O(nm) FP64 storage. Extra traffic is O(n²) per application versus O(n³) factorization, usually favorable at large `n` and a few iterations, but many basis steps can consume the saved factor time. MAGMA has an explicit FP32 factor copy, residual workspace, and (for GMRES) high-precision basis. By comparison, cuSOLVER’s caller allocates the bufferSize-reported workspace; retained factor and Krylov-vector lifetimes are opaque. Its optional residual-history table is small relative to an N-by-N matrix, while internal GMRES/work storage may not be. Both designs pay extra reads of `A` and factor data instead of treating correction as a free arithmetic patch. No memory traffic or occupancy result here predicts another GPU.

## Why the acceptance test can work, and when it cannot

For a nonsingular fixed linear system, one step has a meaningful residual `r_k=b-Ax_k`; a low-precision LU factorization supplies an approximate preconditioner for a correction equation. Classical iterative refinement converges under sufficient conditioning assumptions often summarized as `κ(A) u_low < 1`, with further requirements on backward-stable factorization and high-enough precision residuals. This is a sufficient-regime summary, not a universal sharp cutoff. Carson and Higham’s three-precision analysis gives one concrete sufficient range: with FP16 factorization, FP32 working precision, and FP64 residuals, classical IR can attain about FP32 accuracy for `κ₂(A) ≤ 10⁴`; GMRES-IR broadens the analyzed range substantially (the solver paper summarizes roughly `κ∞(A)<10⁸` for FP16/FP32/FP64). The theorem depends on the named arithmetic, factorization, residual and GMRES assumptions. High condition number amplifies backward error into forward error, so a small residual alone does not promise small solution error for ill-conditioned `A`. [Carson and Higham, three-precision error analysis](https://eprints.maths.manchester.ac.uk/2562/1/paper.pdf)

Scaling is not cosmetic. FP16’s small exponent range can overflow, underflow, or round away entries before useful correction begins. The MAGMA TC-AIRS study uses two-sided diagonal scaling to equilibrate rows and columns, a scalar scale to fit range, and optional diagonal perturbation only in its SPD factorization variant; preprocessing time is included in end-to-end measurements. In its reported general LU comparison, two-sided diagonal scaling was applied. cuSOLVER’s public IRS docs do not promise the same equilibration; callers must decide whether and how to scale, and then preserve the transformation when mapping the answer back.

An actual counterexample is MAGMA’s published `ramage02` Navier–Stokes matrix, order 16,830, `κ∞=4.52×10⁸`, measured on a system equipped with Quadro GV100 PCIe and Quadro RTX 8000 GPUs. In the two-sided-diagonally-scaled table, FP16-TC→FP64 GM refinement took 83 iterations and 0.96 s; the FP64 `dgesv` reference took 0.76 s, so the mixed path was 1.26× as long (listed speedup 0.79×). This is not a theoretical concern or a synthetic GEMM cancellation screen: correction work erased the factorization gain in a named solver/problem. The same study reports up to 4× speedup for well-behaved dense cases, but also a harder synthetic case where classical FP16-TC IR did not converge while GM and IRGM did. cuSOLVER’s 3–4× FP64-to-FP16 figure is its expectation from large-matrix rank-k throughput, not a disclosed benchmark result. Neither result is a Xe2 forecast. [Haidar et al., measured GPU TC-AIRS study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7735315/)

## Transfer boundary to neural-network forward passes

A model forward pass does not inherit this theorem by analogy. Iterative refinement assumes one fixed linear equation, repeatedly evaluates its residual against the same `A` and `b`, applies a correction operator, and stops when a normwise backward-error bound is met. A transformer forward pass is a composition of nonlinear maps: attention softmax, normalization, gated MLPs, discrete top-k expert choices, activation quantization, and recurrent state updates. Rounding an activation changes later inputs and sometimes the route itself; there is no single global residual `b-Ax`, low-precision LU preconditioner, or contraction test certifying the final logits. Local linear algebra kernels may use their own error bounds, but those do not compose into the solver guarantee. This report is reusable numerical-method knowledge, not an inference precision recommendation.

## Evidence limits and source ledger

MAGMA v2.5.0 source confirms the FP64-input mixed FP16-TC path, FP32 persistent factor, and GMRES helper; its tensor-core update uses 16-bit operands. The published performance study does not identify a commit, so its timings are not results for this source pin. NVIDIA cuSOLVER 13.4 is closed source: documentation pins its interface, not private scratch layout or a source SHA. The designs share refinement mathematics but differ in factorization, scaling, storage, GMRES choices, and defaults. [pinned MAGMA v2.5.0 implementation note](https://github.com/icl-utk-edu/magma/blob/630c968a10c4d67a6e679ba942561520160bfc5d/README_FP16_Iterative_Refinement.txt)

The MAGMA/Tensor-Core study used V100-class hardware and CUDA 10.1; it does not establish Xe2/SYCL performance or transformer quality. cuSOLVER has no public source revision to pin. No code was copied and no Strata measurement is made.
